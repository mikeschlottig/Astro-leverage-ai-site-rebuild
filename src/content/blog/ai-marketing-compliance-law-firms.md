> **TL;DR**
>
> - ABA Model Rule 7.1 bars false or misleading communications, and it applies to website copy, bios, blog posts and chatbot output alike.
> - ABA Formal Opinion 512 (July 2024) makes verifying AI-generated claims the attorney's non-delegable duty.
> - Florida and California have added specific rules for AI in 2026. No U.S. attorney had been publicly disciplined solely for AI-generated marketing copy as of September 2026, but the research expects first enforcement within about 18 months.
> - A workable process: attorney sign-off on every factual claim, case result and citation; chatbot screening and disclosure; a verification log.

This is general information about marketing practice, not legal advice. Advertising rules differ by state, and your own bar's rules and ethics counsel govern what your firm may publish.

## What Model Rule 7.1 actually prohibits

Rule 7.1 prohibits a lawyer from making false or misleading communications about the lawyer or the lawyer's services. The standard applies equally to website copy, attorney biographies, blog articles and AI chatbot output. Related rules matter too: Rule 5.3 on supervising nonlawyer assistants, Rule 1.6 on confidentiality and Rule 1.1 on technological competence.

The practical meaning for marketing is plain. If a sentence about the firm's results, experience or the law is wrong, or implies more than is true, the lawyer answers for it, even when a vendor or a tool wrote it.

## Formal Opinion 512: the duty to verify

ABA Formal Opinion 512, issued in July 2024, addresses lawyers' use of generative AI. Together with the rules above, it places on the attorney non-delegable personal responsibility for supervising external marketing agencies and independently verifying every factual claim, case result, statute citation and comparative statement an AI tool produces before publication.

Courts have shown how seriously they take unverified AI output. Sanctions in *Mata v. Avianca* (S.D.N.Y. 2023), which involved fabricated case citations, are the line most often cited.

## The state patchwork

- **Florida.** Florida Bar Ethics Opinion 24-1 (January 19, 2024) addresses generative AI. The Florida Supreme Court amended Rule 4-1.1 on technological competence in 2024, with a three-hour technology CLE requirement, and in May 2026 amended Rule 2.515 so that AI-fabricated citations are sanctionable across Florida courts.
- **California.** The State Bar issued Practical Guidance on generative AI in November 2023, followed by ethics code revisions in May 2026 covering competence, client communication, confidentiality, candor and supervision of AI, including agentic AI. California also enacted SB 574.
- **Everywhere else.** Rules differ. Check your own state bar.

## Chatbot traps

Florida's advertising rules (Subchapter 4-7, Rule 4-7.13) show what regulators worry about when a firm puts an AI assistant on its website or phone line. The firm should:

- screen visitors and limit the AI's communication if the person is already represented by another attorney
- clearly disclose that the intake assistant is not a lawyer and keep it from giving specific legal advice that could form an attorney-client relationship or amount to unauthorized practice of law
- include a clear, conspicuous disclaimer when an advertisement uses an AI-generated voice or image that could suggest the speaker is a lawyer or firm employee

## Privacy and privilege

Data handling is its own risk. In *United States v. Heppner* (S.D.N.Y. 2026), a federal court held that materials a defendant generated with a public consumer AI tool, without counsel's direction, had neither attorney-client privilege nor work-product protection. A federal court in Colorado (March 30, 2026) barred confidential material from consumer AI platforms lacking contractual safeguards. When you choose a website platform or AI tool, check whether its terms say prompts and chat transcripts are not used to train models, and what the retention period is. The research notes one popular small-firm platform whose general terms were silent on both points.

## The 2026 enforcement forecast

As of September 2026, no U.S. attorney had been publicly disciplined solely for AI-generated marketing blog copy. Legal ethics analysts nonetheless note that the machinery is active: courtroom citation sanctions, bar complaints, consumer protection actions, search engines' scaled-content abuse penalties and private liability all reach unverified AI copy. The research infers that high-profile enforcement is likely within the next 18 months. That is an inference, not a prediction anyone can guarantee. The sensible response is not alarm. It is a process.

## A compliant AI content workflow

1. **Draft with a human in the loop.** AI may assist drafting; a person owns the result.
2. **Source every factual claim.** Each statistic, statute and case citation gets a source link in the working draft.
3. **Attorney review.** The responsible attorney checks every claim, result and comparison, and approves in writing.
4. **No outcome promises.** Do not guarantee results, and do not publish case results without the attorney's confirmation and required disclaimers.
5. **Disclose.** Mark AI voices and bots clearly and screen for existing representation.
6. **Log it.** Keep a record of who verified what, and when.

## Verification logs: your evidence file

A short log per published page makes the process provable: page URL, date, claims checked and their sources, reviewing attorney and approval date. If a complaint arrives, the log shows the firm acted reasonably.
