If your firm's website lives on a platform you lease, you may not own it. That has been true for years. A change in who owns the platform is a good moment to find out exactly where you stand.

This article describes a market development and a checklist. It is not legal advice, and we are not saying any vendor's contract is improper. Read your own contract.

"Don't know" is the most common answer, and it is the one that costs the most to discover during a dispute.

## How we got here

The 1977 U.S. Supreme Court ruling in *Bates v. State Bar of Arizona* opened lawyer advertising and started the long commercialization of legal marketing. Over the following decades, dominant vendors built firm websites on closed, proprietary content management systems and leased the domain, content and hosting to the firm under multi-year contracts.

Industry practitioners describe leaving such a provider as a bad divorce: the firm loses the site architecture, the accumulated backlinks and the search rankings, and rebuilds from zero. By 2026, market awareness has largely ended that model among sophisticated buyers.

## The buyout, explained

On October 3, 2024, Thomson Reuters signed a definitive agreement to sell its FindLaw business, including the Super Lawyers rating service, to Internet Brands. The transaction closed December 2, 2024.

Internet Brands is backed by the private equity firms KKR and Warburg Pincus and already owned Avvo, Martindale-Hubbell, Lawyers.com and Nolo. With FindLaw added, nearly the whole legacy U.S. legal directory infrastructure sat under one private-equity-backed owner. Only Justia and independent rating publications remained outside it.

## What the ownership transfer did to service

Market commentary and client migration reports published after the sale describe account management becoming more transactional, communication slowing and service customization narrowing, and describe widespread dissatisfaction among firms buying bundled website and SEO retainers from FindLaw or sister properties between 2025 and 2026. These are reports and commentary, not audited service metrics, and individual experiences differ.

## The exit checklist

1. **Domain.** Confirm the registrant is the firm, with credentials the firm controls.
2. **Content.** Export every page, bio and blog post in a usable format. Confirm in writing that the firm owns the copy.
3. **Backlinks.** List the sites linking to you and which pages they point to. This list drives the redirect plan.
4. **Google Business Profile.** Make sure the firm is the owner, not the vendor.
5. **Ad and analytics accounts.** Google Ads, Analytics 4 and Search Console should be in the firm's name with the vendor as a user, not the reverse.
6. **Contract.** Note term, notice period and what the vendor says happens to the site on exit.

## Migration without losing rankings

Rankings are lost when URLs change without redirects, when content thins out in the rebuild and when entity data breaks. Build a one-to-one redirect map from every old URL to its new equivalent, carry over the substance of high-performing pages, and keep your name, address and phone identical on every listing during the move. Launch with the map live, then watch Search Console for crawl errors.

## What a modern ownership guarantee looks like

Leading buyers now ask for a written contractual guarantee that the firm retains 100% ownership of portable, open-source code (WordPress, Webflow or React front ends), the written copy, the Google Business Profile and the paid ad accounts.
